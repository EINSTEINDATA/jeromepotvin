The data is there.The foundations aren’t.

Why so many analytics and AI projects hit a wall, and how to see it coming before it gets expensive.

Jerome Potvin (Jay)Director, Commercial Strategy – Innovair SolutionsOctober 2026

“Just give us the budget by customer. One line, two or three tweaks.” Over my career, in organizations of very different sizes and industries, I’ve heard this request dozens of times. On paper, nothing complicated. In practice, you often only need to dig a little to discover that the data model simply cannot answer the question correctly.

Companies have never had access to so much data. Artificial intelligence has never offered so many ways to use it. And yet one question remains: can we truly trust the decisions we draw from it?

That’s the paradox: we want to accelerate with AI, while the foundations of our data don’t always hold up. The technology is available. So is the data. What is too often missing is the architecture, the governance and the understanding needed to connect that data to a reliable decision.

01

The request

It all starts with a legitimate request: understand performance in order to make a better decision. The problem is that the solution is often assumed to be simple before the people responsible for the data have had a chance to assess whether it’s feasible.

An overall budget exists. Leadership now wants to see it by customer, by product, by territory or by channel. But having a total available in no way guarantees that its components are consistent and connected to one another.

I’ll admit it: I too have believed the exercise would be quick. It was by examining the links between tables that I discovered where the real difficulty lay.

02

What lies beneath the line

Beneath a seemingly solid number, you sometimes find inconsistent identifiers, incomplete reference data and missing or incorrect relationships between tables. The dimensions meant to give the numbers meaning no longer allow them to be analyzed together reliably.

A customer can be coded differently from one system to another. A product can change category mid-year. A territory may not have the same definition from one report to the next. Taken individually, each of these gaps seems minor. Together, they weaken the decision.

A real-world example

The same customer exists under three codes, created at different times by three different teams. Its sales are split across three lines; none of them makes the top-20 accounts list. The most strategic customer in the territory becomes invisible, and no one notices, because the total itself is correct.

So we know how much we sold, but not always precisely to whom, in which category or in which territory. Without that granularity, how do you prioritize opportunities, understand margin erosion or build a credible forecast?

This is not an isolated case. According to Gartner, inconsistency of data across sources is the hardest data quality problem to fix, and 59% of organizations don’t even measure the quality of their data. What isn’t measured isn’t managed.

03

Anatomy of a wall: seven layers, one cascade

When a “simple” request fails, we instinctively look for a technical glitch. Yet the problem was usually built long before the report was produced, layer after layer, from the initial request all the way up to the AI ambitions.

For each layer, three angles: what we see, what’s really happening, and what it leads to next.

Layer1
BELOW GROUND LEVEL

Layer 1 — The request

What we see:
a clear, urgent request, framed as “two or three tweaks.”
What’s really happening:
the request was shaped without the people who know the data. No one questioned its feasibility; the result is expected before anyone has looked at the basement.
What it leads to:
the problem is discovered at delivery time, where it costs the most. The expert becomes the bearer of bad news rather than a partner in the solution.
Layer2
BELOW GROUND LEVEL

Layer 2 — The knowledge gap

What we see:
the question that keeps coming back: “Why is this taking so long?”
What’s really happening:
it’s not a lack of intelligence, it’s a blind spot. Someone who knows neither the source systems, nor the structure, nor how the data is organized cannot gauge the effort behind a single line. They don’t know what they don’t know.
What it leads to:
unrealistic expectations and frustration on both sides. Above all, what can’t be seen is underestimated, and so it doesn’t get invested in.
Layer3
BELOW GROUND LEVEL

Layer 3 — Strategy confused with tactics

What we see:
the phrase “data strategy” comes up in every meeting.
What’s really happening:
the term is used to cover a series of actions: a report, a dashboard, an extract. But a strategy answers different questions. Which decisions do we want to make better? What data do they rely on? Who is accountable for it?
What it leads to:
tactical moves pile up without building anything lasting. Each new request starts almost from scratch, and the next one will hit the same wall.
Layer4
BELOW GROUND LEVEL

Layer 4 — Scattered master data

What we see:
no one can say clearly who is responsible for customer or product data.
What’s really happening:
master data management is spread across several functions. Each one handles it in its own way, without an overall view, and structural decisions are made piecemeal. Gartner observes the same thing: executives agree that data quality matters, but don’t see it as their responsibility.
What it leads to:
rules diverge from one function to another and, when a link breaks, no one is tasked with fixing it.
Layer5
BELOW GROUND LEVEL

Layer 5 — Roles seen as secondary

What we see:
tasks related to reference data are seen as administrative, even incidental.
What’s really happening:
because they aren’t recognized as strategic, these roles are given to people who have neither the training, nor the time, nor the mandate to fully take them on.
What it leads to:
this is the central paradox: the foundations of all analytics are laid by the roles the organization values least.

Layer6

Layer 6 — The broken dimensional model

What we see:
you get a total, but it can’t be broken down.
What’s really happening:
dimension tables no longer link properly to the facts or to each other. This isn’t an isolated failure: it’s the technical consequence of layers 4 and 5, accumulated decision after decision.
What it leads to:
you’re stuck at the aggregate level. The questions that really matter, by customer, by product, by territory, go unanswered.
Layer7
VISIBLE

Layer 7 — Analytics and AI on hold

What we see:
analytics and AI projects that stall midway or deliver results no one trusts.
What’s really happening:
descriptive, predictive and prescriptive analytics, like machine learning, all rely on those same dimensions. AI isn’t the culprit: it inherits the foundations it is given.
What it leads to:
a promise that never materializes, and the temptation to blame the tool rather than the basement.

This cascade reveals an uncomfortable truth: the failure shows up in the dashboard, but it almost always originates further upstream, in management decisions, poorly defined responsibilities and day-to-day processes. Fixing a report is pointless if the organization keeps repeating the same mistakes at the source.

04

The real cost: the analytics that never arrive

The cost isn’t limited to the hours spent rebuilding a budget. It shows up in delayed analyses, fragile forecasts, poorly targeted sales opportunities and, more insidiously, in the trust that erodes between teams. When every department has “its” version of the number, meetings are spent reconciling data rather than deciding.

The numbers confirm the urgency. Gartner predicts that through 2026, organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects unsupported by AI-ready data. In a 2024 survey, 63% of organizations said they either did not have, or were unsure whether they had, the right data management practices for AI.

And the bill is already running: Gartner estimates that poor data quality costs organizations at least US$12.9 million a year on average (2020 research).

AI can accelerate analysis, forecasting and recommendations. But it doesn’t magically turn inconsistent reference data into reliable truth.

“Automating bad logic simply produces a wrong answer faster, and with more confidence.”

Before investing in the upper floor, let’s make sure the basement holds.

05

“We can’t afford it“: the most expensive myth

“We can’t afford a data strategy.” I hear it often, especially in small and mid-sized businesses. Yet a strategy doesn’t require an expensive platform. It starts with clear choices: which decisions do we want to improve, which data is essential, and who is accountable for it?

Meanwhile, the gap is widening. In Québec, 12.7% of businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services in the 12 months preceding the second quarter of 2025. That rate climbs to 26.1% among businesses with 100 or more employees, compared with 12.2% among those with 1 to 4 employees. In construction, it tops out at 2.3%.

Among Québec businesses that made no significant investment in their digital infrastructure over the past three years, cost tops the list of obstacles (27.2%). But uncertainty about the return (19.3%) and a lack of specialized expertise (14.3%) follow close behind. Two of the three main barriers are therefore less about money than about knowledge: knowing what it will pay back, and knowing how to do it. We’re back to layer 2.

Company size changes the scale of the means, not the need for foundations. The right starting point isn’t sophistication: it’s discipline. In practice, a small business can achieve a lot in 90 days.

The 90-day plan

DAYS 1–30

Choose and appoint

Pick a single business decision to improve (for example, prioritizing accounts to grow) and appoint an owner for customer and product data, with a written mandate.

DAYS 31–60

Clean what matters

Merge duplicate customers, complete product categories and set a few shared creation rules. Not the whole reference base: only what feeds the chosen decision.

DAYS 61–90

Measure and lock in

Track three simple indicators (duplicate rate, codes without a category, sales not tied to a valid customer) and add a check whenever a new record is created.

It’s not spectacular. That’s exactly why it works.

06

The lessons I take away

If I had to translate this experience into concrete actions for leaders, I would keep seven priorities, one for each layer of the cascade.

  1. Look at the basement before you promise.Before accepting a “simple” request, confirm that the dimensions connect and that you can drill down to the granular level.
  2. Involve the people who know the data from the start.A request framed without them starts with a blind spot. And give them time to explain the real effort, in business terms.
  3. Don’t mistake a report for a strategy.Strategy says why and toward what; tactics say how, today.
  4. Give master data an owner.Reference data that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.
  5. Treat these roles as strategic.Those who lay the foundations need the skills, training and recognition that go with it.
  6. Measure quality, invest proportionately, but start now.Every month of waiting widens the cracks.
  7. See AI as the upper floor.It amplifies the quality of the foundations, good or bad.
07

A tool to share: the 7-question self-assessment

Before launching a new analytics or AI project, take ten minutes with your teams to answer these questions. Each “no” flags a risk to examine, not necessarily a project to stop.

1

Are the people who know the data consulted before a request is launched?The request

2

Do decision-makers broadly understand where their numbers come from and what it takes to produce them?Knowledge gap

3

Do we have a written data strategy, tied to specific decisions, beyond one-off reports?Strategy vs. tactics

4

Does master data (customers, products, territories) have a clearly named owner?Master data

5

Do the people who change reference data have the required skills and mandate?Roles

6

Can we drill down from the total to the granular level without manual rework?The model

7

Do we measure our data quality, with indicators tracked over time?AI-ready

Your score

How to read your score

6 or 7 “yes”

Solid foundations. You can accelerate into advanced analytics and AI.

3 to 5 “yes”

Cracks are visible. Target them before any major AI project.

0 to 2 “yes”

Start with the basement. The 90-day plan is a good place to begin.

08

It’s later than we think

The deadline Gartner pointed to, the end of 2026, is no longer a distant horizon: we’re there. Organizations that took the time to strengthen their foundations are starting to reap the benefits. The others are discovering that their AI projects are stalling exactly where their reports already stalled.

We sometimes ask our teams to deliver faster without giving them the means to make what feeds their analyses reliable. Over time, we confuse speed of production with quality of decision.

The real challenge isn’t to own more data or buy one more AI tool. It’s to build an organization that can trust its numbers, and knows why it trusts them.

Before your next dashboard, your next forecast or your next AI project, ask three questions:

  1. Can we connect our data reliably?
  2. Who is accountable for its quality?
  3. Which decision do we really want to improve?

The data is there. It’s up to us to build the foundations.

How many “yes” answers did you get on the self-assessment? And above all: which crack will you fix first?
