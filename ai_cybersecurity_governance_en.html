Artificial Intelligence at the Crossroads of Cybersecurity and Governance

Why manufacturers, distributors, and electrical and plumbing contractors need an independent AI practice — before shadow AI and ungoverned agents turn convenience into the next breach.

By Jerome Potvin · Director, Commercial Strategy · Innovair Solutions

Ask a security leader whether AI changed their risk picture in 2026, and the answer is nearly unanimous. Ask the same organization who owns the AI governance decision — who approved the tools running today, who reviews the agents acting on the company's behalf — and the room usually goes quiet. That gap between adoption and oversight is no longer theoretical. It has a price tag, and in several sectors, it already has victims.

AI is already the most widely deployed defensive tool in the security stack — 77% of organizations use it for threat detection. But that same capability cuts both ways. Seven publicly documented incidents between 2025 and 2026 — an unpatched prompt-injection flaw in Microsoft 365 Copilot, an unsanctioned agent action inside Meta's internal tooling, a Salesforce Einstein AI injection exposing customer data, a compromised open-source LiteLLM supply chain, a ten-hour global ChatGPT outage, a Vercel/Context.ai credential breach, and a malicious VS Code extension that sat undetected for months — all trace back to the same root cause: AI systems deployed faster than the review cycles built to govern them.

"AI is evolving faster than the review cycles organizations were built around." — a pattern echoed across nearly every 2026 governance study reviewed for this piece.

A Governance Architecture Moving at Several Speeds

Three reference frameworks now structure how organizations govern AI, and they are converging on the same three pillars — risk-based classification, human oversight, and accountability — even though only one carries the force of law. The EU AI Act is a binding regulation with extraterritorial reach and tiered penalties. The U.S. NIST AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary but has become the de facto technical benchmark cited in procurement and insurance. Canada's proposed federal AI law died on the order paper and has not been reintroduced, leaving PIPEDA and a voluntary Code of Conduct as the practical floor nationally.

AI Governance Gaps Observed in the Enterprise — 2026
The pattern is consistent: governance exists on paper far more often than it exists in practice.

Sources: IBM, Cost of a Data Breach Report 2026; Larridin, State of Enterprise AI 2026.

Having a framework on paper and having one that functions are different things. 75% of organizations have no formal AI governance policy at all; of those that were breached, 70% had no dedicated AI governance function in place at the time. A framework that exists only in a policy binder does not stop a breach — it only documents, after the fact, that one was possible.

Why an Independent, Cross-Functional AI Practice Becomes Unavoidable

The most commonly cited cause of the gap between policy and practice has a name: shadow AI — tools, models, and browser extensions adopted by employees or business units without security's knowledge or sign-off.

Adoption of "Shadow AI" Inside Organizations — 2026
80% of organizations tolerate moderate-to-pervasive shadow AI. Only 25% report none.

Source: Optro.ai, Shadow AI Stats 2026.

Only 25% of organizations maintain a complete inventory of the AI systems actually running inside their environment. You cannot govern what you have not counted. This is precisely why AI governance keeps failing when it is treated as an add-on to an existing committee — a security team, a legal team, or an innovation lab each see only their own slice of the problem. An independent AI practice, reporting across functions rather than inside one of them, is what closes that structural blind spot: a living inventory of every AI system in use, risk assessment calibrated against recognized frameworks (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001), and formal integration with information security — not parallel, disconnected tracks.

What an independent AI practice actually does

Maintains the living inventory of models, agents, and third-party AI tools · classifies each system by risk tier before deployment, not after · owns the relationship with information security rather than duplicating it · sets the baseline metrics a board can actually audit · and is the single point that can say yes or no to a new AI use case, instead of five departments each assuming someone else already did.

Canada and Quebec: No Federal Floor, a Provincial One Already in Force

Canada has no binding federal AI law. The Artificial Intelligence and Data Act died when Parliament was prorogued and has not returned. In that vacuum, Quebec's Law 25 is what actually applies today — fines of up to $25 million or 4% of worldwide revenue, whichever is greater, for organizations doing business in the province regardless of where they are headquartered. With no national floor, every Canadian organization has to build its own — and for organizations operating across provinces or borders, that means designing to the strictest applicable standard rather than waiting to see which jurisdiction moves first.

Sector Focus: Manufacturing, Electricity, Distribution, and Plumbing

The abstraction ends the moment you look at operational technology. In IT environments, an AI governance failure usually means a data leak. In OT environments — the plant floor, the substation, the delivery fleet — it can mean physical disruption.

Manufacturing: the #1 Target of Industrial Ransomware — 2025-2026
67% of industrial ransomware victims in 2025 were manufacturers, driven largely by IT/OT convergence.

Sources: Dragos, Industrial Ransomware Analysis 2025-2026; Verizon DBIR 2026; Ponemon Institute 2025.

⚙️ Manufacturing

Manufacturers are the single most targeted sector for industrial ransomware, precisely because IT/OT convergence means a phishing email on the office network can now reach a production line. 61% of breaches trace back to a third party or supplier, and 56% of manufacturing OT environments cannot detect adversary activity with the tools they already have installed.

⚡ Electricity & Grids

Hydro-Québec's substations have been publicly described as "prime targets." The state-linked Volt Typhoon group has held dormant access inside North American electrical grids for years — a pre-positioned disruption capability, not a conventional data-theft risk. AI-driven grid optimization tools expand that attack surface further if deployed without the same rigor applied to safety-critical systems.

🚚 Distribution

Logistics and distribution cyber incidents rose 61% in 2025. A breach at a single distributor doesn't stay contained — it propagates to hundreds of downstream contractor and retailer accounts that trusted that one vendor's systems.

🔧 Plumbing & Electrical Contractors

Smaller contracting businesses rarely see themselves as a target, but they inherit governance risk from every distributor and manufacturer in their supply chain, while running their own AI-powered scheduling, quoting, and CRM tools with little to no oversight of what those tools do with customer and job-site data.

What changes, and what doesn't: the technology differs by sector, but the governance discipline is identical — know what AI is running, classify it by risk, name an accountable owner, and integrate with security rather than working around it.

Insights and Lessons Learned

  • AI governance is a cross-functional problem masquerading as three separate ones — security, compliance, and innovation each own a piece, and none owns the whole.
  • A policy on paper is not a control. The organizations that were breached mostly already had a stated AI policy; what they lacked was a function actually enforcing it.
  • Shadow AI is the symptom, not the disease. It appears wherever governance is slower than the business need it's meant to serve.
  • OT environments change the stakes from data loss to physical and safety consequences — governance frameworks built for office IT do not transfer automatically.
  • In a patchwork regulatory environment (no Canadian federal law, a binding EU standard, sector-specific U.S. enforcement), building to the strictest applicable standard beats waiting for legislation to catch up.
  • An independent AI practice is what converts governance from a document into an operating discipline — because it is the only structure with visibility across every function that touches AI.

Interpreting the Result

0–8 — FragileThe organization is operating without a safety net. Start with questions 1–4 (inventory) before any other initiative: you cannot govern what you cannot see.
9–16 — DevelopingSome foundations exist but remain fragmented. Prioritize naming a single owner of AI governance and segmenting IT/OT if the organization has an operational or industrial component.
17–24 — MatureMaintain vigilance through regular exercises (question 12) and track evolving regulatory frameworks — the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, and Quebec's Law 25 among them.

Recommended External Tools and Resources

  • CyberSecure Canada — a federal cybersecurity certification program designed for SMBs, backed by free training from the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security; the most realistic entry point for trade businesses (plumbing, residential electrical) and small distributors.
  • NIST AI RMF Playbook — a free, interactive tool for structuring an AI risk-management program around the four functions Govern, Map, Measure, Manage.
  • ISO/IEC 42001 — for organizations seeking independent third-party certification of their AI management system, useful for reassuring clients or partners.
  • ISA/IEC 62443 — the reference standard for any industrial, energy-distribution, or connected-building environment; treat as a priority for manufacturing, electricity, distribution, and building trades.
  • Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (cyber.gc.ca) — free sector-specific guides, including for critical infrastructure and small organizations.

Conclusion

None of this argues against adopting AI faster. It argues for building the one structure that lets an organization do so without finding out, after the fact, which tool nobody approved. AI governance is no longer a project you assign to an IT department and revisit next year. It's a condition of resilience — for the office network, the plant floor, and the grid alike.
