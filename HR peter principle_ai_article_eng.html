Promoted to Fail: Why the Peter Principle Is About to Meet Its Match — and It Isn't HR

By Jerome Potvin · Director, Commercial Strategy · Innovair Group (ED Canada Group)

Every organization has one: the person who rose fast, sits in a title that outpaces their comfort zone, and knows it more than anyone else does. For decades, HR has had a name for this — the Peter Principle — and no real answer for it. This isn't about pointing fingers; almost everyone ends up here at some point in a career. AI might be the first force that actually changes the equation — not by exposing people, but by finally giving everyone, at every level, a clearer, kinder way to know where they stand and what to build next.

The Principle Nobody Wants to Say Out Loud

In 1969, Laurence J. Peter wrote it as satire: in a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to their level of incompetence. It was meant to be a joke. It didn't stay one. Decades of organizational research have since found real evidence behind it — most starkly in sales organizations, where top performers are routinely promoted into management roles they are not equipped for, producing measurable productivity losses for the whole team, not just the individual.

The mechanism is almost innocent. We promote people for being good at their current job, then hand them a role that requires an entirely different skill set — usually people leadership instead of individual output — and act surprised when it doesn't transfer.

of managers report being promoted for the wrong reasons — tenure, likeability, or past output rather than leadership readiness. The measured result: an 11-point drop in team engagement. Source: Forbes/Gallup analysis, 2026.

To be clear: none of this is a knock on the person in the role. Most people who end up over their head got there by saying yes to an opportunity, being loyal, and doing exactly what got rewarded. The system asked them to keep climbing the same ladder instead of asking if it was still the right ladder. That's a design problem, not a character flaw — and it's fixable.

The Uncomfortable Twin: Fear of the "Unqualified" Success

There's a second, quieter anxiety running through HR departments right now, and it's almost the mirror image of the Peter Principle. It's the employee with no diploma, no formal credential, sometimes no obvious technical training — who outperforms colleagues with the "right" pedigree. Every organization has one of these too, and they tend to make people nervous in a very specific way: if credentials don't predict performance, what exactly are we screening for?

The data says this fear is well-founded, and the shift is already structural, not anecdotal. Employees without a four-year degree now stay with companies 34% longer than degree-holders, and IBM's "New Collar" program has spent years hiring technicians without degrees and training them internally — a model Google, Delta Air Lines, and Bank of America have since followed for large swaths of their workforce.

But the honest version of this story includes the gap between announcement and action. Research from Harvard Business School and the Burning Glass Institute found that at some large firms, fewer than 1 in 700 new hires were actually non-degree holders, even after the degree requirement was formally dropped. The policy exists on paper. Sourcing, assessment, and manager incentives — the parts that actually move hiring — often don't change.

The Fear That Runs the Other Way

It's easy to assume the anxiety only flows one way — toward the self-taught employee without a diploma. In practice, HR leaders quietly worry just as much about the opposite case: the highly credentialed hire who looks perfect on paper. And the worry isn't snobbery in reverse — it's backed by real evidence, and it deserves the same fair hearing as the story above.

The research on overqualification is remarkably consistent: employees who feel their credentials exceed the job's demands report lower growth satisfaction, which is one of the strongest predictors of them actually leaving — not eventually, but within a few years. It isn't disloyalty; it's an unmet need to keep growing that the role simply can't feed. Hiring practitioners themselves describe overqualified candidates as more likely to feel underutilized, disengage, and unintentionally challenge supervisors — not out of arrogance, but because a highly-trained mind left idle tends to look for something to fix, including things that were never broken.

Why this matters for your org chart

Turnover is expensive either way. The average cost of losing an employee now sits at roughly $45,000, and organizations with rigid, low-autonomy structures are the ones that lose their most credentialed people fastest — precisely the people who cost the most to replace.

So the honest picture is symmetrical, not one-sided: under-credentialed employees get pre-judged before they start, and over-credentialed employees get quietly managed out of growth once they arrive. Neither fear is really about the diploma. Both are about whether the organization has a real plan for where a capable person goes next.

Where AI Actually Changes the Equation

This is where 2026 looks different from every prior decade of this debate. AI doesn't resolve the Peter Principle by making better guesses about who to promote — it resolves it by making the guessing itself unnecessary. Microsoft already uses AI analytics on email patterns, meeting participation, and cross-team collaboration to identify leadership tendencies before a promotion decision is made — not resume, not tenure, not the last performance review.

The same shift is happening from the other direction. For technical and AI-adjacent roles, LinkedIn finds skills-based matching expands the qualified candidate pool roughly 8.2 times over — largely by surfacing self-taught, non-degree practitioners who were invisible to title-based and pedigree-based search. Opportunity@Work estimates more than 70 million U.S. workers fall into this category: skilled through alternative routes — community college, military service, on-the-job learning — but structurally screened out until now.

Put simply: AI is starting to do two things HR has never been able to do consistently at scale — evaluate actual behavior instead of proxies like degrees and tenure, and surface real competence regardless of where it was built.

The One Thing AI Can't Manufacture: Curiosity

Here's the part that should worry — and motivate — every employee reading this, credentialed or not. AI can now assess competence with more precision than any manager ever could. What it still can't do is install the trait that predicts who stays competent five years from now: intellectual curiosity.

The real dividing line

It's no longer degree vs. no degree, or tenure vs. talent. It's whether someone keeps learning once the title, the diploma, or the early win stops doing the work for them. WEF data already shows 39% of workers' core skills are expected to change by 2030 — meaning almost none of today's competence is guaranteed to still be relevant in five years, regardless of how it was earned.

This reframes the Peter Principle for the AI era. The failure mode was never "unqualified person gets promoted." It was "person stops learning once the promotion arrives." A self-taught engineer who keeps rebuilding their skill set and a newly-minted MBA who coasts on the credential are now on opposite sides of the same line — and AI-era organizations are increasingly built to notice the difference.

The Good News: These Skills Are Learnable, Starting Now

None of this requires reinventing yourself. The skills that hold up best in the next decade are ones almost anyone can build with steady practice — no matter which side of the diploma line you're on today. Research from PwC and the World Economic Forum points to a consistent short list:

  • Working AI fluency. Not coding — comfort using AI tools well: prompting clearly, checking outputs, knowing where they help and where they don't.
  • Data literacy. Reading a dashboard, spotting a trend, asking "what does this number actually mean" before acting on it.
  • Judgment and critical thinking. PwC's 2026 analysis of over a billion job postings found the tasks AI adds to jobs are 2.5 times more likely to lean on judgment, empathy, and creativity — the things AI still can't do for you.
  • Adaptability as a habit, not a slogan. Treating a new tool or process as one more thing to learn, not a threat to prove you're still relevant.
  • People skills — genuinely, not as a soft add-on. Listening, giving feedback well, and managing a room. These are exactly what separates a good manager from a promoted-too-fast one.

Here's the encouraging part: none of these live on a transcript. They're built through practice, feedback, and curiosity — which means the field really is levelling, for the diploma-holder and the self-taught alike.

So Who Actually Wins?

Good news: it's not a contest between the credentialed and the credential-less, and nobody needs to feel behind. The people who come out ahead over the next decade tend to share one thing, regardless of where they started:

  • Employees who treat learning as ongoing, not a milestone. The diploma or the first big win becomes a starting point, not a plateau.
  • Organizations that promote on demonstrated behavior, not proxies. The ones using real assessment — AI-assisted or not — instead of announcing a policy and changing nothing underneath it.
  • Managers who get trained for the job they're promoted into, not just congratulated for the job they're leaving. Structured management training remains the single most-cited mitigation for Peter Principle dynamics in the research.
  • Anyone willing to be evaluated on output instead of pedigree — which cuts both ways: it exposes the underqualified manager coasting on tenure exactly as fast as it surfaces the self-taught contributor everyone underestimated.

What Managers Owe the Process Too

Most of this article is about employees — but the promotion decision itself belongs to someone. If a promotion goes wrong, the fairest question isn't "why didn't they perform," it's "what did we actually evaluate before we promoted them?" A few honest checks any manager can start using this quarter:

  • Separate "good at the job" from "ready for the next one." They're different questions. Ask both, out loud, before the offer goes out.
  • Give the role a trial run before it's permanent. A stretch project, an interim title, or a 90-day review point costs far less than an 11-point engagement drop across a whole team.
  • Train for the new role, not just for the old one's absence. Structured management training remains the single most-cited fix for Peter Principle dynamics in the research — and it's usually skipped entirely.
  • Keep a real second path. If the only way up is management, you'll keep promoting your best specialists out of the work they're actually great at. A dual-track ladder — expert and manager — fixes this at the root.

None of this is about second-guessing every promotion forever. It's about spending fifteen more minutes on the decision that will shape a team for the next two years.

Where Do You Actually Stand? A Two-Minute Check

Not a test to pass or fail — just a few honest questions worth sitting with, whichever side of the diploma line you're on:

  • Have I learned something genuinely new in the last three months — not just done more of what I already know?
  • When a new tool or process shows up, is my first reaction curiosity or resistance?
  • Could I name, right now, the one skill that would matter most for the role I want next?
  • Do I ask for feedback before I need it, or only after something's gone wrong?

If most answers came easily, you're already doing the thing this article is actually about. If one made you pause — that's not a red flag, that's just where to start.

Conclusion: Lessons Learned

  • The Peter Principle was never really about diplomas. It was about promoting for the wrong evidence — past output instead of future-role readiness.
  • Fear of the "unqualified" high performer is usually fear of an outdated proxy, not a real signal that standards have dropped.
  • Policy without mechanics changes nothing. Dropping a degree requirement on paper moved the needle by roughly 3.5 percentage points in practice — most companies that "went skills-based" barely changed who they actually hired.
  • AI's real contribution isn't smarter hiring algorithms — it's removing the excuse to keep guessing when better evidence of actual competence is now measurable.
  • Curiosity is the only credential that doesn't expire. In a decade where 39% of core skills are expected to shift, the diploma tells you what someone knew. Curiosity tells you what they'll still know.
