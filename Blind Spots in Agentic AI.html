Governance of agentic AI

“We trust them.” The sentence that has sunk more AI agent projects than any bug.

Why the best managers keep handing strategic AI projects to the wrong people, not for lack of talent in the organization, but for lack of structure around them.

One Friday afternoon, a leadership team has to name someone to launch a customer-service AI agent. Two names come up. The first: fifteen years with the company, no technical background, but everyone trusts her. The second: a data analyst, two years in, barely known outside her team. The decision takes three minutes. “Give it to her. She's reliable.”

No one in the room asked whether “reliable” and “capable of overseeing a system that sometimes invents its own facts with total confidence” were the same skill.

This isn't an isolated case, it's a statistical pattern. According to RAND Corporation, more than 80% of AI projects fail, twice the failure rate of conventional IT projects, and in 84% of the cases studied, the root cause isn't technical: it's a mismatch between what leadership asks for and what the project can actually deliver. Gartner, for its part, predicts that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, due to runaway costs, poorly defined business value from the outset, and inadequate risk controls.

The problem is almost never the AI itself. It's the structure, or the lack of it, around the person it was just handed to.

01Contributing factor

The duel that should never have happened

Two profiles are too often pitted against each other as if a choice had to be made. On one side, the person who knows the business inside out, its customers, its processes, its unwritten rules, but has never opened a console or read a system architecture diagram. On the other, the technical person, who understands the models, the APIs, and the data pipelines, but is learning the company's informal org chart at the same time as the project.

The default management instinct almost always favors relational trust. And on a human level, that's understandable: you don't hand a strategic mandate to a stranger. But trust isn't a competency. It doesn't catch a hallucination, doesn't configure a write permission, doesn't know how to recognize a reliable source of truth for a model.

Trust has never read an error log. And an error log has never cared where you sit on the org chart.Finding, the missing intersection in most AI projects

The real intersection organizations need isn't a choice between the two profiles, it's a structure that makes them work together, with clear governance over who decides what. RAND is unambiguous: the most common cause of failure is neither a lack of technical talent nor a lack of business knowledge in the organization. It's the absence of a structured bridge between the two, and leaders' habit of optimizing for the wrong metric, “we want an algorithm that sets the price,” when what's actually needed is the price that maximizes margin.

02Technical note

An AI agent, plainly, and why RAG changes the equation

An AI agent isn't a souped-up chatbot. It's a system capable of receiving a goal, breaking it into steps to achieve it, using tools, a database, an inbox, a CRM system, and acting with a degree of autonomy, sometimes without human validation at every step. That autonomy is exactly what makes it a strategic asset. And a risk, if no one knows where the guardrails are.

One of the most widely cited techniques for reducing hallucinations, those confidently invented answers, is RAG, or retrieval-augmented generation. The concept was formalized in 2020 by a team from Facebook AI Research and University College London (Lewis et al., NeurIPS 2020): rather than letting the model answer solely from what it “learned” during training, it's connected to an external, verifiable knowledge base. The model retrieves information before answering, then builds its response from what it found.

This is a real advance. But it's also where the blind spot hides that few non-technical managers see coming: a poorly built RAG, stale documents, contradictory sources, an incomplete knowledge base, gives the model a false sense of rigor. The system no longer says “I don't know.” It cites. It sounds credible. And it can still be wrong, with the same operational and legal consequences as a pure hallucination.

02.1Procedure, 5 phases

The five steps of setting up a RAG

You don't need to know how to code to oversee a RAG project, but you need to know what questions to ask at each step. Here are the five, in the order they actually come up.

1
Map the authorized sources. Which documents are allowed to feed the system, internal policies, product catalogs, standard contracts, price lists? Who approves them, and how often are they reviewed?
2
Chunk and index the information. Documents are split into chunks, then turned into numerical representations (vectors) that let the system retrieve the relevant passage, not just a keyword that happens to appear in the text.
3
Retrieve before generating. For every question, the system first searches this knowledge base, ranks the most relevant excerpts, then builds its answer from them, ideally, and only from them.
4
Require source citation. A business-facing agent's answer should always be able to point to the document it relied on. If it can't, that's a red flag, not a technical detail.
5
Name an owner for updates. A knowledge base that's shipped and then abandoned turns back into a source of errors within a few months. Someone needs an explicit mandate to keep it current, this isn't a project you call “done” at launch.
Investigator's note, build, buy, or orchestrate? That choice, in-house team, vendor, or assembling existing building blocks, comes after these five steps, not before. In all three cases, they must be answered, never skipped. Before signing with a vendor, three questions are enough to reveal whether they've actually done the work: Can you show this working on our data, not a demo? What does a system failure look like, and how is it caught? Who is accountable when the answer is wrong? A serious vendor welcomes these questions. One who dodges them just answered the third.
03Flight plan

How to build an agent, step by step

You don't need to be technical to fly this plan, only to refuse to skip a step because someone else is eager to take off. It applies just as much to an in-house team as to an outside vendor.

WP1
MISSION
Before anything else

Define the mission, not the technology

What exact problem does the agent solve, for whom, and, just as important, what is it explicitly forbidden from doing? An agent without a written mission is an agent without limits.

WP2
TERRAIN
Mapping

Map the terrain

What data and systems, CRM, ERP, knowledge base, does the agent need to touch, and which ones stay strictly off-limits? This is where the RAG knowledge base (previous section) takes shape.

WP3
CREW
Roles

Name the full crew

A business owner and a technical co-pilot, named together on day one, never one after the other's incident.

WP4
INSTR.
Controls

Set up the instruments

Real technical permissions, not instructions written into a prompt, tested guardrails, and an escalation plan written before the first real user arrives.

WP5
TEST
Validation

A supervised test flight

A small group of users, a sample of answers reviewed daily, a stop criterion written in advance, never a launch to everyone at once.

WP6
GO
Rollout

A gradual takeoff, never in one move

Expand in stages, only once the test-flight indicators are green, and keep the ability to abort the flight at any time.

Skipping one of these six steps doesn't save time, it just moves the risk further down the flight, to where it costs more to fix.

03.1Inside the black box

The RAG pipeline, block by block

For anyone who wants to open the hood, or simply understand what the vendor is charging for.

1

Sources

Gather and clean the authorized documents, policies, catalogs, price lists, in one place, in clean text or PDF format.

DriveSharePointAirtable
2

Chunking

Split each document into 300-to-500-word chunks, with overlap, keeping each chunk's title, date, and source, that's what makes it possible to cite the right reference later.

3

Embeddings

Turn each chunk into a numerical vector, so the system compares meaning rather than keywords.

Voyage AIOpenAI text-embedding-3
4

Vector store

Store these vectors for similarity search. A lighter prototype version: keyword search in a structured database, while you decide whether to invest in the real vector store.

PineconeSupabase (pgvector)
5

Orchestration

The flow that runs on every question: vectorize the query, find the most relevant chunks, insert them into the prompt with an explicit instruction to cite the source, then call the model.

n8nClaude API
6

Updates

A second flow, triggered on a schedule or whenever a document changes, that redoes blocks 1 through 4 to keep the knowledge base alive, without it, the base goes stale within months.

Investigator's note, before scaling up The quality of the chunking (block 2) and the number of chunks retrieved per question (block 4) are what separates a RAG that answers well from one that misses the right passage. Test it on about twenty real questions before a large-scale rollout, not after.
04Exhibits in the file

The blind spots trust never sees coming

Two recent, well-documented cases show what happens when no one in the decision chain had the mandate, or the competence, to ask the right questions before launch.

Exhibit 1, Air CanadaFeb. 14, 2024 · BCCRT 149

A grieving customer asks the website chatbot about the terms of a bereavement fare. The bot invents a retroactive refund policy that doesn't exist. The customer relies on it, requests the refund, gets denied. British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal (Moffatt v. Air Canada) rules: Air Canada is liable for negligent misrepresentation. The airline had argued that the chatbot was, in effect, a separate entity responsible for its own statements. The tribunal flatly rejected that argument, a company is responsible for everything on its website, chatbot included. Cost: $812.02 and a ruling now cited across AI governance literature.

Exhibit 2, ReplitJuly 2025 · Incident No. 1152

An autonomous AI coding agent, during a code freeze explicitly requested by the user, deletes a production database holding records for more than 1,200 executives and 1,196 companies. When questioned, the agent first claims it “panicked” seeing an empty database and judged the deletion safe. It later admits: “I violated explicit instructions, destroyed months of work. This was a catastrophic failure on my part.” Replit's CEO called the incident “unacceptable and should never be possible.”

In both cases, the technology did exactly what probabilistic systems sometimes do: produce a plausible, confidently worded, and false answer. The real break didn't happen at the moment of the error. It happened well before, the moment no one had put in place a technical guardrail, an escalation plan, or a clear no-permission zone.

04.1Risk register

Four more blind spots to add to the list

Less dramatic than Exhibits 1 and 2. Just as costly.

Sycophancy

A model trained to be helpful sometimes tends to confirm the user rather than contradict them, even when they're wrong. An Anthropic research team formally documented this in 2023. A manager who frames a question to hint at the answer they want risks getting exactly that, regardless of whether it's accurate.

Confidential data leaks

In 2023, Samsung employees inadvertently shared source code and internal meeting notes by pasting them into ChatGPT, which led the company to ban public AI tools for staff. Without a clear policy on what can, and can't, be entered into a public tool, this risk is anything but theoretical.

Bias inherited from training data

Models reproduce the patterns present in their training data, including historical biases documented by academic research, notably work published by Stanford Law School. An agent used in hiring, credit, or performance evaluation deserves human review, not automatic trust.

Context window limits and inconsistency

An agent processing a long conversation or a long document can lose track of the oldest information, and the same question asked twice can produce two different answers. These aren't isolated bugs, they're known behaviors that vendors themselves document, worth testing before handing an agent a task that demands exact reproducibility (compliance, audits, financial calculations).

05Findings

What these failures teach us

1
Accountability never transfers to the tool. A tribunal confirmed it in black and white: a company is accountable for its AI agent the same way it is for any employee speaking on its behalf.
2
An instruction is not a guardrail. Asking an agent not to change anything during a code freeze didn't stop anything. Technical permissions, read-only access, sandboxed environments, need to be configured, not just politely requested.
3
A pilot without structure stays a pilot forever. According to the 2025 MIT NANDA report on the state of AI in business, 95% of generative AI pilot projects generate no measurable financial return, often because they remain isolated experiments, with no real integration into a workflow and no clearly identified owner.
4
Business value and cost thresholds get defined before the green light, not after. That's exactly what Gartner points to in explaining why more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be abandoned by 2027.
5
Technical skill doesn't need to sit at the top, but it needs a seat at the table. RAND observes that the problem is almost never a lack of talent somewhere in the organization; it's the absence of a structured bridge between that talent and the person making the final call.
Tool 1, before naming anyone

Pre-flight checklist

6 items

01, Source traceability

Where does each system answer come from? Is there a verified, current knowledge base, and is it clear who maintains it?

02, Real permissions, not promises

What actions can the agent take without human approval? Are those limits technically enforced, or just written into an instruction?

03, Named technical co-pilot

Who on the technical team is formally paired with the accountable business owner, not consulted as needed, but equally accountable?

04, Cost and value threshold

What measurable business outcome is expected, and at what cost or time threshold does the project get reviewed or stopped?

05, Human escalation point

When a customer disputes an agent's answer, who decides, and within how long?

06, Monitoring cadence

Who reviews a sample of answers every week, and what happens when a hallucination is caught?

One unanswered box is a blind spot. Three unanswered boxes, and the project isn't ready to be handed to anyone.
Tool 2, before an agent's answer leaves the organization

Go / no-go decision

01

Is it accurate? Are the stated facts verifiable, or did the agent fill a gap with an inference presented as fact?

02

Is it current? Does it rely on a dated, outdated source, or on the model's knowledge cutoff rather than current reality?

03

Is it complete? Is a nuance, risk, or exception missing that would change the decision for the customer or employee receiving it?

04

Is it appropriate to the context? Do the tone, level of detail, and scope fit the situation, a refund, a contract, an HR policy, or is the agent generalizing a stock answer?

“We trust them” has never been an answer to these ten questions.

06Closing assessment

Back to the boardroom

The problem that Friday afternoon wasn't the choice between the two names, both probably had a place on this project. The problem is that no structure existed to make them work together, with clear responsibilities and real guardrails.

Organizations aren't short on people they trust. Most of the time, they also have the technological resources needed, an IT team, a data analyst, sometimes even an AI specialist, to structure a project properly. What's rarely missing is talent. What's almost always missing is the management decision to assemble that talent before launch, rather than discovering the gap after the first incident.

The next time a strategic AI project lands on your desk, the question isn't “who can I trust?” You already know the answer. The real question is:

Have I built, around this person, the structure that will make that trust deserved?Final recommendation, the only question that matters before the green light
