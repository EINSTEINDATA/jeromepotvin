AI Alone Won't Make Your Company More Productive

Statistics Canada just proved it with hard numbers. A world tour, a Canada tour, and a stop in Quebec — built from 47 verified sources — on why the "AI premium" melts away the moment you count the foundations underneath it.

By Jerome Potvin · Director, Commercial Strategy · Innovair Solutions

Ask any distributor which line item on the budget is labeled "AI." Now ask which line item is labeled "fixing our product data." The second one usually doesn't exist — even though, according to Statistics Canada, it's the difference between a real productivity gain and a rounding error.

The "AI Premium" Melts Once You Count the Foundations
The raw productivity gap between AI adopters and non-adopters shrinks by two-thirds once prior productivity is controlled for — and disappears into statistical noise once analytics, cloud, and R&D capacity are accounted for.

Source: Statistics Canada, April 2026 — productivity gap, adopters vs. non-adopters.

That's not an argument against AI. It's an argument against believing AI is the variable doing the work. The companies showing up as "AI winners" in the data were already ahead on the boring stuff — clean data, cloud infrastructure, an R&D habit — before the model ever touched a workflow.

Almost Everyone Uses AI. Almost No One Profits From It.

McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey, spanning 1,719 respondents across 97 countries, lays out the drop-off in stark terms.

The Paradox
Each stage of scaling loses more than half the field. By the time you reach measurable profit, six companies in a hundred are left standing.

Source: McKinsey, The State of AI, August 2026 — 1,719 respondents, 97 countries.

Eighty-nine percent of organizations use AI somewhere. Forty-four percent scale it beyond a single team. Thirty-seven percent see any measurable effect on EBIT. Six percent get five points or more of EBIT from it. That funnel is the real story of 2026 — not adoption, which is functionally universal, but the collapse between adoption and value.

Canada's Wholesalers Adopt AI at Poland's Pace

Zoom out to a world tour and the picture sharpens further. Statistics Canada and Eurostat don't use identical definitions, so the comparison is directional rather than exact — but the direction is not flattering.

World Tour: AI Adoption Rate by Market
Canada's wholesale trade sector — the segment electrical and HVAC distributors sit in — trails Canada's own national average, and adopts at roughly Poland's rate.

Sources: Eurostat 2025 (10+ employees) · Statistics Canada, Q2 2026. Definitions differ between jurisdictions.

Denmark leads at 42%. The EU average sits at 20%. Canada, across all businesses, comes in at 19.2% — respectable, until you isolate wholesale trade specifically, where the rate drops to 7.9%, just under Poland's 8.4%. Distribution is adopting AI more slowly than the rest of the Canadian economy, in a segment where product data — SKUs, specs, cross-references, pricing — is the actual raw material AI needs to be useful.

Quebec Trains the World's AI Minds. Ontario Just Overtook It.

Quebec has spent a decade building AI research capacity — Mila, IVADO, a research ecosystem other provinces study rather than compete with. That head start shows up in talent and research output. It does not show up, anymore, in business adoption.

The Champion's Paradox: Quebec vs. Ontario Adoption
Ontario has closed the gap and, on stated intentions, is set to pull ahead — despite Quebec's research advantage.

Source: Institut de la statistique du Québec, November 2025. Ontario 2024 figure derived (13.3% − 7.8 points).

In Q2 2024, Quebec led Ontario 9.4% to an estimated 5.5%. By Q2 2025, Ontario had already pulled even at 13.3% against Quebec's 12.7%. On stated intentions going forward, Ontario projects to 16.5% while Quebec edges up to only 13.1%. Research leadership and business-adoption leadership, it turns out, are two different races — and Quebec is currently winning the one that produces papers, not the one that produces adopters.

None of the capital flowing into compute and sovereign infrastructure is wasted — Canada needs that capacity. But for a distributor, the bottleneck was never a shortage of compute. It's that the product catalogue an AI tool would read from is still full of duplicate SKUs, inconsistent specs, and cross-references nobody has cleaned up since the last ERP migration. No press release announces funding for that. Every company waiting on it has to fund it themselves.

Conclusion: AI Pays Off to the Extent a Data Strategy Exists

Put the pieces from Statistics Canada, Gartner, and McKinsey side by side and a formula emerges — not a precise equation, but a working model for where the value actually comes from.

AI return ≈ data strategy × AI-ready data (per use case) × redesigned workflow (measured)
One factor at zero, the product is zero.

A data strategy makes a company 15 percentage points more likely to adopt AI at all, per Statistics Canada. AI-ready data, prepared per use case rather than in general, is the difference Gartner points to when 60% of AI projects get abandoned for lack of it. And McKinsey's research shows three-quarters of high performers redesign the underlying workflow before they call an AI deployment done — against roughly a quarter of everyone else. Skip any one of the three, and the return on the model itself — however good the model is — rounds to zero.

Lessons Learned

  • The AI premium is real, but it belongs mostly to companies that were already ahead on data, cloud, and R&D — not to the model itself.
  • Canadian wholesale distribution adopts AI more slowly than the Canadian economy overall, in the one segment where clean product data is the actual product.
  • Public AI investment in Canada is concentrated in compute and sovereign infrastructure — not in the data clean-up that determines whether that compute gets used well.
  • The fastest, cheapest first move costs nothing: ask your best counter rep what they don't trust in the system, and fix that first.
